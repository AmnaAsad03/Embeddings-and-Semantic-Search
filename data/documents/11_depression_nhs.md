# Depression

## Overview

Depression can affect adults, children, and young people. It can develop slowly, and a person may not always recognize that they are feeling or behaving differently.

Feeling low or depressed from time to time is common. However, if low mood lasts for 2 weeks or more or begins to affect everyday life, it can be a sign of depression.

Depression can range from mild to moderate or severe depending on the symptoms, how long they last, and how much they affect daily life.

## Symptoms

Typical symptoms of depression may include:

- Feeling sad or hopeless for most of the day, nearly every day
- Losing interest in things that were previously enjoyable
- Feeling tired or exhausted
- Disturbed sleep or sleeping more than usual
- Changes in appetite
- Feeling worthless or guilty
- Difficulty concentrating or making decisions
- Thoughts of death or suicide
- Feeling unable to cope with everyday life

Other signs may include:

- Irritability or anger
- Slower speech and movements
- Feeling restless or fidgety
- Low confidence or self-esteem
- Becoming isolated from other people
- Poor personal hygiene or reduced care of oneself or the home

### Depression in Children and Young People

Symptoms in children and young people may include:

- Persistent sadness or low mood
- Irritability or frequent grumpiness
- Losing interest in activities they previously enjoyed
- Frequent tiredness or exhaustion
- Sleeping difficulties or sleeping more than usual
- Difficulty concentrating
- Reduced interaction with friends and family
- Indecisiveness
- Low confidence
- Eating less or overeating
- Significant changes in weight
- Difficulty relaxing or unusual lethargy
- Feelings of guilt or worthlessness
- Feeling empty or emotionally numb
- Thoughts about suicide or self-harm
- Self-harm

Children with depression may also experience anxiety, headaches, or stomach aches. Problems at school or changes in behaviour can also be signs of depression.

Older children who are depressed may misuse drugs or alcohol.

### Postnatal Depression

Postnatal depression can occur after having a baby. Symptoms may include:

- Low mood
- Difficulty enjoying anything
- Feeling hopeless or unable to cope
- Feeling guilty or worthless
- Feeling like a bad parent
- Feeling fearful or anxious
- Restlessness or irritability
- Difficulty sleeping even when there is an opportunity to rest
- Difficulty concentrating or making decisions
- Difficulty bonding with the baby
- Thoughts of suicide or harming oneself or the baby

Symptoms can begin during pregnancy, soon after birth, or up to a year after the baby is born.

Feeling down, anxious, or irritable for a few days after giving birth can be common and is sometimes called the baby blues. It usually improves within 2 weeks. If symptoms continue, become worse, or become difficult to cope with, help should be sought.

## Causes

There is no single cause of depression. It can develop because of a mixture of genetic, biological, psychological, and life-related factors.

Possible contributing factors and triggers include:

- Family difficulties
- Bullying
- Physical, emotional, or sexual abuse
- Family history of depression or other mental health problems
- Relationship problems
- Financial problems
- Health problems
- Bereavement
- Problems at school or with other people
- Recent or past traumatic experiences

### Causes and Risk Factors for Postnatal Depression

The exact cause of postnatal depression is not fully understood. Factors that may contribute include:

- Stress associated with caring for a new baby
- Lack of sleep
- Hormonal changes during pregnancy

Risk factors include:

- Depression or another mental health condition before or during pregnancy
- Recent difficult life events
- Having a premature or unwell baby
- Lack of social support
- Relationship problems
- Domestic violence or abuse

## Risk Factors

Factors associated with an increased risk of depression include:

- Family history of depression or other mental health problems
- Difficult life events
- Abuse or trauma
- Bullying
- Family or relationship difficulties
- Financial difficulties
- Health problems
- Lack of social support
- Previous depression or other mental health problems

Risk factors can vary depending on the type of depression.

## Diagnosis

A healthcare professional may ask questions about:

- How the person has been feeling
- The symptoms they are experiencing
- How long the symptoms have lasted
- How the symptoms affect everyday life

For postnatal depression, a GP, health visitor, or midwife may ask questions about symptoms and may ask the person to complete a questionnaire.

The severity of depression may be described as mild, moderate, or severe based on symptoms, duration, and impact on daily life.

## Treatment

Treatment for depression can include:

- Talking therapies
- Cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT)
- Counselling
- Antidepressants
- Self-help approaches

Treatment should be discussed with a healthcare professional to determine what is appropriate.

### Treatment for Postnatal Depression

Treatment may be provided by a GP, talking therapies service, or specialist community perinatal mental health team.

Treatments include:

- Talking therapies such as CBT
- Antidepressants

Many antidepressants can be used during breastfeeding. A healthcare professional can prescribe a medicine considered suitable for someone who is breastfeeding.

Postnatal depression usually improves within 3 to 6 months with treatment, although it can sometimes last longer.

### Treatment for Psychotic Depression

Severe depression can sometimes occur with psychosis. This is known as psychotic depression.

Treatment may include:

- Antipsychotic medicines
- Antidepressants
- Talking therapies such as CBT
- Social support for needs such as education, employment, or accommodation

Some people may need a short hospital stay while receiving treatment.

Electroconvulsive therapy (ECT) may sometimes be recommended for severe depression when other treatments, including antidepressants, have not worked.

Follow-up appointments are usually required so that the person can be monitored.

## Prevention and Self-Management

The NHS provides several approaches that may help people manage depression or low mood:

- Stay connected with friends and family
- Talk about feelings with trusted people or healthcare professionals
- Take regular exercise
- Eat a healthy, balanced diet
- Maintain regular meals
- Try to maintain a regular daily routine
- Get enough sleep and rest
- Avoid using alcohol or drugs to cope with symptoms
- Set small, achievable goals
- Try mindfulness techniques
- Spend time doing enjoyable activities
- Seek support when needed

Regular exercise can help improve mood, particularly for people with less severe depression. Adults are generally advised to work towards 150 minutes of moderate-intensity activity each week, building up gradually if they have not exercised recently.

## Complications

Depression can significantly affect everyday life and may become a long-term problem.

Possible serious effects described by the NHS include:

- Social isolation
- Problems with daily functioning
- Substance misuse in some young people
- Thoughts of suicide
- Self-harm

Severe depression may also be associated with psychosis, including delusions and hallucinations.

People with psychotic depression have an increased risk of suicidal thoughts.

## When to Seek Medical Care

If low mood or depressive symptoms continue for 2 weeks or more or begin to affect everyday life, seek medical help.

People who have been feeling depressed for some time or who do not feel like themselves should discuss their symptoms with a GP or another appropriate healthcare professional.

Children and young people who may be depressed should be assessed by a GP or appropriate mental health service.

If someone develops thoughts that their life is not worth living, thoughts of suicide, or thoughts of self-harm, they should seek help immediately.

If psychotic symptoms are causing possible risk of harm to the person or others, urgent mental health or emergency medical help is required.

## Additional Information

### Psychotic Depression

Some people with severe depression may experience psychosis. Psychotic depression involves symptoms of depression together with psychotic symptoms.

Psychotic symptoms may include:

- Delusions: beliefs that are unlikely to be true
- Hallucinations: experiencing things that are not actually present, including hearing voices

The delusions and hallucinations often reflect the person's deeply depressed mood.

Psychomotor agitation, involving difficulty relaxing, sitting still, or constant fidgeting, can occur. Some people may instead experience psychomotor retardation, where thoughts and physical movements become slowed.

The exact reason why some people with severe depression develop psychosis is not known. Genes and difficult life experiences may contribute.

### Supporting Someone With Depression

A person with depression may not recognize that they need help. Friends, relatives, or carers may notice changes first.

Helpful approaches include:

- Letting the person know that you care and are willing to listen
- Avoiding judgment
- Encouraging healthy activities and self-care
- Encouraging the person to speak with a GP or mental health professional
- Staying in contact through messages, phone calls, or visits
- Being patient
- Encouraging appropriate professional or community support

People supporting someone with depression should also take care of their own mental health.
