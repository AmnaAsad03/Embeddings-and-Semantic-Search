# Diabetes

Also called: Diabetes mellitus, DM

## Overview

Diabetes, also known as diabetes mellitus, is a disease in which blood glucose, or blood sugar, levels are too high. Glucose is the body's main source of energy. The body can make glucose, but it also comes from the food a person eats.

Insulin is a hormone made by the pancreas. Insulin helps move glucose from the bloodstream into cells, where it can be used for energy.

In diabetes, the body may not make insulin, may not use insulin as well as it should, or both. As a result, too much glucose remains in the blood instead of reaching the cells. Over time, high blood glucose levels can lead to serious health conditions. Managing diabetes can help control blood glucose and reduce the risk of these health problems.

## Types

There are different types of diabetes:

### Type 1 Diabetes

In type 1 diabetes, the body makes little or no insulin. It occurs when the immune system attacks and destroys the cells that produce insulin.

### Type 2 Diabetes

Type 2 diabetes is the most common form of diabetes. The body may still be able to make insulin, but the cells do not respond well to insulin and cannot easily take up enough glucose from the blood.

### Gestational Diabetes

Gestational diabetes develops during pregnancy. It occurs when the body cannot make the extra insulin needed during pregnancy.

## Symptoms

Symptoms of diabetes may include:

- Feeling very thirsty
- Feeling very hungry
- Urinating more often, including at night
- Fatigue
- Blurry vision
- Numbness or tingling in the feet or hands
- Sores that do not heal
- Losing weight without trying

Symptoms can vary depending on the type of diabetes:

- **Type 1 diabetes:** Symptoms usually develop quickly and can be severe.
- **Type 2 diabetes:** Symptoms often develop slowly over several years and may be so mild that they are not noticed.
- **Gestational diabetes:** Often has no symptoms. When symptoms occur, they may be mild. During pregnancy, screening is usually performed between 24 and 28 weeks.

## Causes

The different types of diabetes have different causes.

### Type 1 Diabetes

Researchers think type 1 diabetes is caused by genes and environmental factors that may trigger the disease.

### Type 2 Diabetes

Type 2 diabetes is caused by several factors, including lifestyle factors and genes. Lifestyle factors include not being physically active and being overweight or having obesity.

### Gestational Diabetes

Researchers think gestational diabetes is caused by hormonal changes during pregnancy along with genetic and lifestyle factors.

## Risk Factors

### Type 1 Diabetes

- Can develop at any age, although it most often starts in childhood.
- Having a parent or sibling with type 1 diabetes may increase the chance of developing it.

### Type 2 Diabetes

Risk is higher for people who:

- Are overweight or have obesity.
- Are over age 35. Children, teenagers, and younger adults can also develop type 2 diabetes, although it is more common in middle-aged and older adults.
- Have a family history of diabetes.
- Have prediabetes, meaning blood glucose is higher than normal but not high enough to be classified as diabetes.
- Previously had gestational diabetes.
- Have given birth to a baby weighing 9 pounds or more.
- Are African American, American Indian, Asian American, Hispanic/Latino, or Pacific Islander.
- Are not physically active.
- Have certain other health conditions, such as high blood pressure or polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS).

### Gestational Diabetes

Risk is higher for people who:

- Are overweight or have obesity.
- Have a family history of diabetes.
- Had gestational diabetes during a previous pregnancy.
- Have given birth to a baby weighing 9 pounds or more.
- Have polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS).
- Are African American, Hispanic/Latino, American Indian, Alaska Native, Native Hawaiian, or Pacific Islander.

## Diagnosis

A health care provider uses one or more glucose blood tests to determine whether a person has diabetes.

Several types of glucose blood tests may be used, including the A1C test.

## Treatment

Treatment for diabetes focuses on managing blood glucose levels.

### Type 1 Diabetes

People with type 1 diabetes need daily doses of insulin, either by injection or through a special pump. Some people may also need another type of diabetes medicine that works with insulin.

### Type 2 Diabetes

Some people with type 2 diabetes may be able to manage or even reverse the condition through lifestyle changes, including:

- Eating a healthy diet.
- Staying at a healthy weight.
- Getting regular physical activity.

Some people also need diabetes medicines to manage their condition.

### Gestational Diabetes

Gestational diabetes may be managed by eating a healthy diet and getting regular exercise. Treatment options should be discussed with a health care provider.

Gestational diabetes usually goes away after giving birth, but having it increases the risk of developing type 2 diabetes later.

### Blood Glucose Monitoring

Checking blood glucose levels is an important part of diabetes management. The appropriate method and frequency of blood glucose monitoring should be discussed with a health care provider.

## Prevention

### Type 1 Diabetes

Type 1 diabetes cannot be prevented.

### Type 2 Diabetes

Type 2 diabetes may be delayed or prevented through lifestyle changes such as:

- Eating a healthy diet.
- Staying at a healthy weight.
- Getting regular physical activity.

### Gestational Diabetes

The same lifestyle changes may also help prevent gestational diabetes.

## Complications

High blood glucose levels over time can lead to serious health conditions.

Managing diabetes and controlling blood glucose can help reduce the risk of these health problems.

## When to Seek Medical Care

People with diabetes should work with their health care provider to determine appropriate treatment and blood glucose monitoring.

Pregnant people are usually screened for gestational diabetes between 24 and 28 weeks of pregnancy.

Treatment plans may differ depending on the type of diabetes and individual circumstances.

## Additional Information

Diabetes management may involve lifestyle changes, blood glucose monitoring, medicines, insulin therapy, and regular interaction with health care providers. The specific approach depends on the type of diabetes and the individual's needs.
