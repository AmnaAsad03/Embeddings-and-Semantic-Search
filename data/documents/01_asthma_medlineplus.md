# Asthma

## Overview

Asthma is a chronic (long-term) lung disease that affects the airways, the tubes that carry air in and out of the lungs. In people with asthma, the airways can become inflamed and narrowed. This can cause wheezing, coughing, and tightness in the chest.

When asthma symptoms become worse than usual, it is called an asthma attack or flare-up. Asthma attacks may develop gradually or suddenly and can sometimes be life-threatening.

## Symptoms

Common symptoms of asthma include:

- Chest tightness
- Coughing, especially at night or early in the morning
- Shortness of breath
- Wheezing, which causes a whistling sound when breathing out

Symptoms can range from mild to severe. Some people experience symptoms every day, while others experience them only occasionally.

During an asthma attack, symptoms become much worse. Severe asthma is associated with more frequent and potentially serious attacks. People experiencing asthma attacks may need their treatment adjusted.

## Causes

The exact cause of asthma is unknown. Genetics and environmental factors likely influence who develops asthma.

Asthma attacks can occur after exposure to an asthma trigger. Triggers are substances or conditions that can start or worsen asthma symptoms. Triggers vary from person to person and can change over time.

### Allergic Asthma

Allergic asthma is triggered by allergens, which are substances that cause an allergic reaction. Common allergens include:

- Dust mites
- Mold
- Pets
- Pollen from grass, trees, and weeds
- Waste from pests such as cockroaches and mice

### Nonallergic Asthma

Nonallergic asthma is triggered by factors that are not allergens, including:

- Cold air
- Certain medicines
- Household chemicals
- Respiratory infections such as colds and flu
- Outdoor air pollution
- Tobacco smoke

### Occupational Asthma

Occupational asthma can occur after breathing in chemicals or industrial dusts in the workplace.

### Exercise-Induced Asthma

Exercise-induced asthma occurs during physical exercise, particularly when the air is dry.

## Risk Factors

Factors that can increase the risk of developing asthma include:

- Exposure to secondhand smoke during pregnancy or early childhood
- Exposure to chemical irritants or industrial dusts at work
- Having a parent with asthma, particularly the mother
- Having other conditions such as obesity or allergies
- Frequently having viral respiratory infections during early childhood
- Sex-related differences: asthma is more common in boys during childhood and more common in women during adolescence and adulthood
- Certain racial or ethnic backgrounds are associated with a higher risk of asthma

Asthma can affect people of all ages, although it often begins during childhood.

## Diagnosis

A health care provider may use several methods to diagnose asthma, including:

- Physical examination
- Medical history
- Lung function tests, including spirometry, to assess how well the lungs work
- Tests that measure how the airways respond to specific exposures, including allergens or medicines that may tighten the airway muscles
- Peak expiratory flow (PEF) testing to measure how quickly a person can force air out of the lungs
- Fractional exhaled nitric oxide (FeNO) testing to measure nitric oxide levels in exhaled breath. High levels may indicate inflammation in the lungs.
- Allergy skin or blood tests when there is a history of allergies, to identify allergens that cause an immune response

## Treatment

People with asthma work with a health care provider to develop a treatment plan. The plan aims to control symptoms and prevent asthma attacks.

### Avoiding Triggers

Treatment may include strategies to avoid known asthma triggers. For example, people whose asthma is triggered by tobacco smoke should avoid smoking and should not allow others to smoke in their home or car.

### Quick-Relief Medicines

Short-term or quick-relief medicines help prevent or relieve symptoms during an asthma attack. These may include an inhaler that should be carried at all times and other fast-acting medicines that help open the airways.

### Control Medicines

Control medicines are taken every day to help prevent asthma symptoms. They work by reducing inflammation in the airways and preventing airway narrowing.

### Treatment Adjustment

Health care providers may adjust treatment until asthma symptoms are adequately controlled.

### Bronchial Thermoplasty

For some adults with severe, uncontrolled asthma that does not respond adequately to other treatments, a provider may consider bronchial thermoplasty. This procedure uses heat to shrink smooth muscle in the lungs. Reducing the amount of smooth muscle can decrease the airways' ability to tighten and may make breathing easier.

Bronchial thermoplasty has risks, so its potential benefits and risks should be discussed with a health care provider.

## Prevention

Asthma attacks can be reduced by identifying and avoiding personal triggers and following an asthma treatment plan.

Examples of trigger-avoidance measures include:

- Avoiding tobacco smoke
- Reducing exposure to known allergens
- Avoiding workplace chemicals or industrial dusts when they trigger symptoms
- Managing exposure to cold air, air pollution, respiratory infections, or other known triggers
- Taking prescribed control medicines as directed

Because triggers differ between individuals, identifying personal triggers is an important part of asthma management.

## Complications

Asthma symptoms can become severe during an asthma attack. Severe attacks can sometimes be life-threatening.

People with severe or uncontrolled asthma may experience recurrent asthma attacks and may require changes to their treatment.

## When to Seek Medical Care

Emergency medical care is needed when a severe asthma attack occurs and short-term relief medicines do not work.

People who experience asthma attacks may also need to contact their health care provider because their asthma treatment may need to be changed.

## Additional Information

Asthma can affect people differently, and symptoms and triggers may change over time. Effective management generally involves identifying triggers, following an individualized treatment plan, using prescribed medicines appropriately, and working with a health care provider to maintain control of symptoms.
