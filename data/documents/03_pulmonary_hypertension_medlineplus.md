# Pulmonary Hypertension

**Also called: Pulmonary arterial hypertension**

## Overview

Pulmonary hypertension, sometimes called PH, is a serious condition that affects the blood vessels in the lungs. It develops when the blood pressure in the lungs is higher than normal.

The heart pumps blood to the lungs so they can add oxygen to the blood. The blood then returns to the heart and travels to the rest of the body so that tissues can get the oxygen they need.

Blood moves from the heart to the lungs through blood vessels called pulmonary arteries. If the pulmonary arteries become damaged, narrowed, or blocked, blood does not flow through them as well. This can increase the blood pressure in the arteries and cause pulmonary hypertension.

There are different types of pulmonary hypertension, including pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH). The different types are based on what caused the disease.

## Symptoms

The symptoms of pulmonary hypertension can be difficult to recognize because they are similar to symptoms of other medical conditions. As a result, it can sometimes take years for someone to be diagnosed.

Symptoms may include:

- Shortness of breath
- Chest pain or pressure
- Dizziness that may lead to fainting
- Fatigue
- Swelling of the abdomen, legs, or feet
- Heart palpitations, meaning the feeling that the heart is pounding or beating too fast

## Causes

Pulmonary hypertension can develop on its own or be caused by another medical condition. Sometimes the cause is unknown or unclear.

Possible causes include:

- **Heart diseases**, including left-sided heart failure and congenital heart disease
- **Lung diseases**, such as:
  - Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD)
  - Interstitial lung disease
  - Emphysema
  - Sleep apnea
- **Other medical conditions**, such as:
  - Liver diseases
  - Sickle cell disease
  - Pulmonary embolism, which involves blood clots in the lungs
  - Connective tissue disorders such as scleroderma

## Risk Factors

Certain factors can increase the likelihood of developing pulmonary hypertension:

- **Age:** The risk increases with age. The condition is usually diagnosed between ages 30 and 60.
- **Environmental exposure:** Exposure to asbestos or certain infections caused by parasites can increase risk.
- **Family history and genetics:** Certain genetic disorders, including Down syndrome, congenital heart disease, and Gaucher disease, can increase risk. A family history of blood clots can also increase risk.
- **Lifestyle habits:** Smoking and illegal drug use can increase the risk of developing pulmonary hypertension.
- **Certain medicines:** Some medicines used to treat cancer and depression can increase the risk.
- **Sex:** Pulmonary hypertension is more common in women than in men.

## Diagnosis

To determine whether a person has pulmonary hypertension, a health care provider may:

- Ask about the person's medical history and symptoms.
- Perform a physical examination, which may include checking blood oxygen levels, listening to the heart and lungs, and checking blood pressure.
- Order diagnostic tests, such as:
  - **Blood tests** to look for blood clots, stress on the heart, or anemia
  - **Heart imaging tests**, such as cardiac MRI
  - **Lung imaging tests**, such as a chest X-ray
  - **Electrocardiogram (ECG or EKG)**

## Treatment

There is often no cure for pulmonary hypertension, but treatments can help manage symptoms. Treatment is based on the person's needs and the cause of the pulmonary hypertension.

Treatment may include:

### Healthy Lifestyle Changes

- Healthy eating, including eating less salt
- Regular physical activity, which may be performed through pulmonary rehabilitation

### Medicines

Medicines may include:

- Blood thinners
- Medicines to control the rate at which blood is pumped throughout the body
- Medicines that relax blood vessels and allow blood to flow more easily
- Diuretics to reduce swelling

### Other Treatments

- Oxygen therapy
- Procedures to reduce pressure in the heart or pulmonary artery
- Lung transplant in some severe cases


## Complications

Pulmonary hypertension can worsen over time and lead to serious health problems, including:

- Anemia, which can result in the body not receiving enough oxygen-rich blood
- Arrhythmias, which are problems with the rate or rhythm of the heartbeat
- Blood clots in the pulmonary arteries
- Bleeding in the lungs
- Heart failure
- Liver damage
- Pericardial effusion, which is a collection of fluid around the heart
- Serious pregnancy complications



## Additional Information

Pulmonary hypertension affects the blood vessels of the lungs and can develop because of problems affecting the heart, lungs, blood vessels, or other parts of the body.

Treatment focuses on managing symptoms and addressing the underlying cause. Depending on the severity and cause, management may involve lifestyle changes, medicines, oxygen therapy, procedures, or lung transplantation.
