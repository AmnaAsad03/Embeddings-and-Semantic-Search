# Drug Safety and Availability

## Overview

The U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) provides drug safety and availability information for consumers and health professionals. The information covers new drug warnings, other safety information, safety-related drug labeling changes, drug recalls, compounded-drug safety information, and shortages of medically necessary drug products.

FDA continues to monitor medicines after approval because widespread or long-term use may reveal safety issues that were not identified during clinical trials. When new safety information becomes available, FDA reviews available evidence and may communicate the risk, require changes to drug labeling or patient information, or take other regulatory actions.

## Drug Safety Communications

FDA Drug Safety Communications (DSCs) provide important information about new safety issues associated with medicines so that patients and health care professionals can make informed treatment decisions.

Post-approval safety monitoring may identify new risks through clinical trials, other studies, case reports, and medical literature. After reviewing the available information, FDA may:

- Issue a Drug Safety Communication about the safety issue.
- Require changes to prescribing information.
- Require changes to patient information or Medication Guides.
- Take other regulatory actions when appropriate.

Drug Safety Communications may describe:

- The newly identified or better-understood safety issue.
- The medicines affected.
- Patients or populations who may be at increased risk.
- Recommendations for patients and health care professionals.
- Changes to prescribing or monitoring practices.
- Regulatory actions taken by FDA.

Drug Safety Communications are therefore an important source of postmarket medication-safety information.

## Drug Alerts and Statements

FDA publishes drug alerts and statements to communicate important and sometimes rapidly developing safety information.

### Alerts

Drug alerts provide updates from FDA's Center for Drug Evaluation and Research (CDER) on fast-moving drug-related safety issues. These may include warnings about specific products, medication errors, unapproved drugs, contaminated products, or other emerging risks.

### Statements

FDA statements provide additional information and agency positions concerning drug safety and related regulatory matters.

Patients and health professionals should review the specific FDA alert or statement for the medicine or product involved rather than assuming that an alert applies to all medicines.

## Drug Safety-Related Labeling Changes

FDA maintains the Drug Safety-related Labeling Changes (SrLC) database to provide information about safety-related updates to FDA-approved prescription drug labeling.

The database contains safety-related labeling changes from January 2016 forward. These changes may result from FDA-required safety labeling changes or from labeling updates associated with approved supplements.

Safety-related changes may occur in sections such as:

- **Boxed Warning**
- **Contraindications**
- **Warnings and Precautions**
- **Adverse Reactions**
- **Drug Interactions**
- **Use in Specific Populations**
- **Patient Counseling Information**
- **Patient Information**
- **Medication Guides**

The SrLC database can be searched by drug name or active ingredient, date range, and labeling section.

The database does not represent every piece of current FDA-approved drug labeling. For the most recent complete labeling, FDA directs users to the Drugs@FDA database.

## Drug Recalls

A drug recall is an action used to remove a defective or potentially harmful medicine from the market or to warn patients and consumers about a potential risk.

FDA describes a drug recall as a voluntary action taken by a company to remove a defective drug from the market or warn patients and consumers about a potential risk.

Drug recalls may be associated with problems such as:

- Product quality issues.
- Contamination.
- Manufacturing problems.
- Defective products.
- Incorrect or potentially harmful product information.
- Other conditions that may create a risk to patients.

Recall information may identify:

- Product or brand name.
- Product description.
- Reason for the recall.
- Company responsible for the recall.
- Recall date.
- Affected products or lots when available.
- Recommended actions.

Patients and health professionals should check the specific recall notice to determine whether a particular product, strength, lot, or formulation is affected.

## Compounding Risk Alerts and Safety Information

FDA provides safety information concerning compounded drugs and publishes compounding risk alerts.

Compounded drugs may present specific safety concerns depending on how the products are prepared, distributed, stored, or used. FDA provides information about identified risks and regulatory or safety actions involving compounded drugs.

Safety information for compounded drugs should be evaluated according to the specific product, facility, safety issue, and FDA communication involved.

## Drug Shortages

Drug shortages can affect the availability of medically necessary medicines.

FDA states that shortages can occur for several reasons, including:

- Manufacturing or quality problems.
- Regulatory delays.
- Shortages of active ingredients.
- Shortages of inactive ingredient components.
- Discontinuation of manufacturing.
- Shipping delays.
- Increased demand.

FDA monitors drug supply situations and works with manufacturers to prevent or reduce the impact of shortages.

The FDA Drug Shortages Database provides information about current and resolved shortages and product discontinuations. When a shortage is listed as current, FDA is aware of the supply situation and is working with manufacturers on efforts to mitigate the disruption.

A local pharmacy being temporarily out of a medicine does not necessarily mean that the medicine is in a nationwide FDA-recognized shortage.

During a shortage, patients should discuss alternative treatment options with a health care professional or pharmacist rather than changing or stopping treatment on their own.

## Medication Errors

FDA monitors and works to prevent medication errors involving CDER-regulated drug products.

A medication error is a preventable event that may cause or lead to inappropriate medication use or patient harm while a medication is under the control of a health care provider, patient, or consumer.

Medication errors may be associated with:

- Confusing drug names.
- Look-alike or confusing packaging.
- Labeling problems.
- Incorrect dosing.
- Confusing prescribing information.
- Similar drug names or formulations.
- Incorrect use of different formulations.
- Other factors involving medication naming, labeling, packaging, or design.

FDA monitors medication-error reports and may take regulatory action when safety risks are identified. Possible actions include:

- Revising product labeling.
- Issuing a Drug Safety Communication.
- Making changes to product names when name confusion contributes to safety problems.
- Other measures intended to reduce medication errors.

Medication errors are monitored for prescription drugs, over-the-counter drugs, generics, biosimilars, and other applicable therapeutic products.

## Postmarket Drug Safety Information

FDA continues to monitor medicines after they are approved and marketed.

Postmarket safety information may include:

- Drug Safety Communications.
- Safety Labeling Change Orders.
- Drug Safety-related Labeling Changes.
- MedWatch safety alerts.
- Adverse event reports.
- Medication error reports.
- Product quality complaints associated with adverse events.
- Postmarketing drug safety evaluations.
- Potential safety signals identified from adverse event monitoring.
- Postmarket requirements and commitments.
- Risk Evaluation and Mitigation Strategies (REMS).

Postmarket surveillance helps FDA identify and evaluate potential safety issues that may emerge after broader use of a medicine.

## FDA Adverse Event Monitoring System (AEMS)

FDA is implementing the FDA Adverse Event Monitoring System (AEMS), formerly known as the FDA Adverse Event Reporting System (FAERS).

The system is being developed to consolidate multiple reporting systems across FDA-regulated product categories and improve the quality, consistency, processing, and analysis of safety reports.

For drugs and therapeutic biological products, the former FAERS database contains reports involving:

- Adverse events.
- Medication errors.
- Product quality complaints that result in adverse events.

AEMS and related postmarket reporting systems support FDA's safety surveillance activities.

### Important Limitations of Adverse Event Reports

An adverse event report does not prove that a medicine caused the reported event.

FDA notes that:

- Reports may contain incomplete information.
- Duplicate reports may occur.
- Reported information may not have been medically verified.
- An event may have another cause, including the underlying disease or another medicine.
- The existence of reports cannot establish that a drug caused an event.
- Reporting data cannot be used by themselves to calculate the incidence or occurrence rate of an adverse event.

Therefore, adverse event reports are an important safety-monitoring signal but should not be interpreted as proof of causation or as a measure of how frequently an event occurs.

## Risk Evaluation and Mitigation Strategies (REMS)

A Risk Evaluation and Mitigation Strategy (REMS) is a drug safety program that FDA may require for certain medicines with serious safety concerns.

The purpose of a REMS is to help ensure that the benefits of a medicine outweigh its risks.

REMS may be designed to:

- Prevent a specific serious risk.
- Monitor a serious risk.
- Manage a serious risk.
- Educate health care professionals.
- Educate patients.
- Reinforce safe-use behaviors.
- Require specific actions or monitoring.

REMS are not required for every medicine and are not intended to address every possible adverse effect. Routine medication risks are generally communicated through FDA-approved prescribing information.

Some REMS may include specific requirements for health care professionals, pharmacies, patients, or health care settings.

## Safety-Related Labeling and Regulatory Actions

FDA may take different regulatory actions when new safety information becomes available.

Possible actions include:

- Adding or strengthening warnings.
- Adding or changing a Boxed Warning.
- Adding contraindications.
- Updating adverse-reaction information.
- Adding or strengthening drug-interaction information.
- Updating information for pregnancy, lactation, pediatric, geriatric, renal, or hepatic populations.
- Changing patient counseling information.
- Updating Medication Guides.
- Requiring a Safety Labeling Change.
- Establishing, modifying, or removing a REMS when appropriate.
- Issuing a Drug Safety Communication or alert.
- Requesting or supporting product recalls.
- Taking other actions based on the identified risk.

The appropriate regulatory action depends on the specific medicine, safety concern, available evidence, and public health risk.

## General Adverse Effects and Safety Risks

This document is a general FDA drug-safety resource and is **not associated with one specific medication**. Therefore, it does not provide a universal list of adverse effects, contraindications, or drug interactions.

The safety profile of a medicine must be evaluated using its current FDA-approved labeling and drug-specific safety communications.

Important safety information may include:

- Serious or life-threatening adverse reactions.
- Newly identified adverse reactions.
- Organ toxicity.
- Severe allergic reactions.
- Clinically significant drug interactions.
- Medication errors.
- Risks in specific populations.
- Risks associated with overdose or inappropriate use.
- Risks requiring special monitoring.
- Risks that result in labeling changes or additional regulatory controls.

Drug-specific adverse effects should not be inferred from FDA adverse-event reports alone.

## Drug Interactions

Drug interactions are medication-specific and are not defined by the general Drug Safety and Availability page.

For a particular medicine, clinically significant interactions should be evaluated using current FDA-approved prescribing information and applicable safety communications.

Safety-related interaction information may be added or strengthened in the **Drug Interactions** section of FDA-approved labeling when new safety information becomes available.

Potential interaction risks may include:

- Increased or decreased drug exposure.
- Increased toxicity.
- Reduced therapeutic effectiveness.
- Increased bleeding risk.
- Effects on cardiac rhythm.
- Central nervous system effects.
- Other clinically significant consequences.

The specific interacting medicines, mechanisms, consequences, and management recommendations must be obtained from the labeling for the individual drug.

## Contraindications

Contraindications are drug-specific.

FDA may add or change contraindications when new safety information demonstrates that a medicine should not be used in a particular situation.

Potential contraindication categories can include:

- Specific diseases or medical conditions.
- Severe organ impairment.
- Previous serious hypersensitivity to the medicine or its components.
- Certain concomitant medicines.
- Specific patient populations or circumstances.

Users should consult the current FDA-approved prescribing information for the specific medicine before determining whether a contraindication applies.

## Use in Specific Populations

Drug safety may differ among specific patient populations.

FDA-approved labeling may contain safety information concerning:

- **Pregnancy:** Potential fetal or maternal risks and available pregnancy-related information.
- **Lactation:** Information concerning drug exposure through breast milk and potential effects on the breastfed infant.
- **Pediatrics:** Approved ages, pediatric safety information, and limitations of available data.
- **Geriatrics:** Safety considerations and potential differences in drug response among older adults.
- **Renal impairment:** Potential effects of reduced kidney function on drug exposure and dosing.
- **Hepatic impairment:** Potential effects of reduced liver function on drug exposure and dosing.

These risks are medicine-specific and should be evaluated using the current labeling for the individual drug.

## Medication Errors and Misuse Potential

FDA's medication-error activities focus on preventable problems that may lead to inappropriate medication use or patient harm.

Medication-error risks may arise from:

- Similar or confusing drug names.
- Look-alike packaging.
- Confusing labels.
- Confusing concentrations or formulations.
- Incorrect dosing instructions.
- Incorrect routes of administration.
- Confusion between different formulations.
- Inadequate or unclear prescribing information.

FDA may respond to identified medication-error risks through labeling changes, safety communications, product-name changes, or other risk-reduction measures.

Misuse, abuse, and overdose risks are specific to individual medicines and should be evaluated using their current FDA-approved labeling and applicable regulatory information.

## Patient Safety and Counseling

Patients should use medicines according to the instructions provided by their health care professional and the current FDA-approved labeling.

General safety principles include:

- Read the medication label and patient information carefully.
- Follow the prescribed dose and schedule.
- Ask a health care professional or pharmacist about unclear instructions.
- Tell health care professionals about other medicines being taken.
- Report suspected serious adverse events.
- Check FDA safety communications and recall information when a safety concern is identified.
- Do not stop or change a prescribed medicine solely because of an adverse-event report without discussing the decision with a health care professional.
- During a drug shortage, discuss alternatives with a health care professional or pharmacist.

Drug-specific counseling instructions should come from the medicine's current FDA-approved labeling.

## When to Seek Immediate Medical Care

The FDA Drug Safety and Availability page is a general regulatory resource and does not provide one universal set of emergency symptoms for all medicines.

Patients should seek urgent medical attention for serious or rapidly worsening symptoms, particularly when they may indicate a severe adverse reaction or other medical emergency.

Examples of potentially serious symptoms can include:

- Difficulty breathing or severe shortness of breath.
- Severe allergic-reaction symptoms such as swelling of the face, lips, tongue, or throat.
- Severe chest pain.
- Loss of consciousness or severe fainting.
- Seizures.
- Severe confusion or sudden changes in mental status.
- Severe or rapidly worsening weakness.
- Other symptoms specifically identified as emergency warning signs in the medication's FDA-approved labeling.

The emergency symptoms associated with a particular medicine should be confirmed using its current prescribing information or FDA safety communication.

## Reporting Adverse Events

Patients, consumers, and health care professionals can report suspected adverse events and medication errors to FDA through its reporting systems.

FDA encourages reporting of suspected safety problems even when the reporter is uncertain whether the medicine caused the event.

Reports contribute to FDA's postmarket safety surveillance and may help identify potential safety signals.

For U.S. drug-related adverse-event reporting, FDA provides the MedWatch reporting system.

**MedWatch:** 1-800-FDA-1088

Adverse-event reports are evaluated as part of FDA's broader safety-monitoring activities. A report does not establish that a medicine caused the event.

## Drug Shortage Safety Considerations

Drug availability can affect patient treatment and medication safety.

When a medicine is unavailable:

- Check the FDA Drug Shortages Database to determine whether a nationwide shortage is recognized.
- Do not assume that a local pharmacy shortage represents a nationwide shortage.
- Contact a pharmacist or health care professional about available alternatives.
- Do not independently change the dose, formulation, or medicine because of a shortage.
- FDA may work with manufacturers to prevent, mitigate, or resolve shortages.

Drug shortage information can change as manufacturers provide updated supply information.

## Important Data Interpretation Notes

This document describes FDA's medication-safety information and regulatory resources. It is not a substitute for drug-specific prescribing information or professional medical judgment.

Important distinctions include:

- A safety communication does not mean that every patient taking the medicine will experience the reported risk.
- An adverse-event report does not prove that the medicine caused the event.
- Adverse-event reporting data cannot by themselves establish event incidence.
- A drug recall applies only to the products, lots, strengths, or formulations identified in the specific recall notice.
- A drug shortage is determined at the relevant national supply level and may not be equivalent to a temporary local pharmacy shortage.
- Safety information can change as FDA receives and evaluates new evidence.
