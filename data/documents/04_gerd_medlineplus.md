# GERD

**Also called: Gastroesophageal reflux disease**

## Overview

Gastroesophageal reflux (GER) occurs when stomach contents move back up into the esophagus, the tube that carries food from the mouth to the stomach. This can happen when the muscle at the end of the esophagus does not close properly. Stomach contents can then move back up and irritate the esophagus.

Having reflux occasionally is common. However, if symptoms occur two or more times a week or cause damage to the lining of the esophagus, a person may have gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD).

GERD is a chronic, long-lasting condition that can cause discomfort and may lead to other health problems over time.

Anyone can have GERD, including infants and children.

## Symptoms

The most common symptom of GERD is **heartburn**, which is a burning feeling in the chest or throat.

GERD can also occur without heartburn. Other symptoms may include:

- Tasting acid or food in the back of the mouth
- Dry cough
- Hoarse voice
- Asthma-like symptoms
- Trouble swallowing

### Chest Pain Warning

Get medical help right away if you have chest pain together with shortness of breath or pain in the jaw or arm. These symptoms can be signs of a heart problem.

## Causes

GERD can occur when the muscle at the bottom of the esophagus becomes weak or relaxes at the wrong time.

Factors that can increase the likelihood of GERD include:

- Being overweight or having obesity
- Pregnancy
- Smoking or exposure to secondhand smoke
- Having a hiatal hernia, in which part of the stomach pushes up into the chest

Certain foods, drinks, or medicines can also make GERD symptoms worse.

## Risk Factors

Factors associated with an increased likelihood of GERD include:

- Overweight or obesity
- Pregnancy
- Smoking
- Exposure to secondhand smoke
- Hiatal hernia
- Certain foods, drinks, or medicines that worsen symptoms

## Diagnosis

A health care provider will ask about symptoms and medical history.

Tests that may be recommended include:

- **Upper GI endoscopy:** Uses a small camera to examine the lining of the esophagus and stomach.
- **Biopsy:** Removes a small tissue sample for examination.
- **X-ray:** Produces images of the upper digestive system.
- **Esophageal pH test:** Measures how often acid backs up into the esophagus and how long the acid remains there.
- **Esophageal manometry:** Checks how well the esophagus moves food during swallowing.
- **Transnasal esophagoscopy:** Uses a small tube with a camera to look for damage to the esophagus.

## Treatment

Most people can manage GERD with lifestyle changes and medicines. In rare cases, surgery may be needed.

### Lifestyle Changes

Lifestyle changes may include:

- Eating smaller meals
- Avoiding lying down for at least 2–3 hours after eating
- Avoiding foods that trigger symptoms, such as spicy foods, chocolate, caffeine, or fatty foods
- Losing weight if needed
- Raising the head of the bed
- Quitting smoking

### Medicines

Medicines used to manage GERD may include:

- Over-the-counter medicines, such as antacids
- Prescription medicines

## Prevention

Symptoms of GERD may be improved or reduced by:

- Avoiding alcohol and foods that trigger symptoms
- Eating smaller meals
- Avoiding eating close to bedtime
- Wearing loose-fitting clothing
- Sleeping on the left side or raising the head of the bed
- Maintaining a healthy weight

## Complications

If GERD is not treated, it can cause problems such as:

- Inflammation of the esophagus
- Scarring of the esophagus
- Changes in the tissue lining of the esophagus, known as Barrett's esophagus
- Worsening of asthma
- Chronic cough
- Chronic hoarseness

## When to Seek Medical Care

Seek medical help right away if chest pain occurs together with:

- Shortness of breath
- Pain in the jaw
- Pain in the arm

These symptoms can indicate a heart problem rather than GERD.

For ongoing or recurring reflux symptoms, a health care provider can evaluate the symptoms and determine whether GERD is present.

## Additional Information

GERD is a chronic condition caused by stomach contents moving back into the esophagus. Occasional reflux is common, but frequent reflux or reflux that damages the esophageal lining may indicate GERD.

Management commonly involves lifestyle changes and medicines. In rare cases, surgery may be required.
